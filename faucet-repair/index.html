What is inside a faucet and why it fails

A faucet is a small valve with parts that wear. Compression faucets use rubber washers that harden. Cartridge faucets use a cylinder that scars or clogs. Ball and ceramic disc faucets use seats and springs that wear with age. Which type you have decides the repair.

Low flow is often a clogged aerator

The small screen at the tip of the spout is the aerator. Mineral flakes and sediment collect there and choke the flow. Unscrewing and cleaning it takes a few minutes. If flow is still weak after cleaning, the cartridge or the supply valve may be the cause. Check whether low flow affects one faucet or the whole house. If it affects the whole house, a larger plumbing problem may be involved, and our leak detection page may help.

Before you call about a faucet

  • Shut the small valves under the sink if the faucet will not turn off.
  • Unscrew the aerator and rinse out any grit.
  • Check the supply lines under the sink for drips or corrosion.
  • Note the brand and finish if you can, since it helps with parts.
  • Keep the old cartridge or part if you can.

Repair or replace the faucet

Many faucets can be repaired for a small part cost. A faucet that is corroded, pitted or built by a brand that no longer sells parts is usually better replaced. When you choose a new faucet, the finish and the mount type matter as much as the brand. We install faucets you supply or help you pick a model that fits your sink. Howell note: hard water and faucet parts

New Jersey American Water's 2025 Coastal North report, which lists Howell as a distribution sampling location, shows total hardness of 72 to 140 mg/L. That is moderate hardness. Over years it leaves scale on aerators and inside cartridges, which cuts flow and makes handles stiff.

Cleaning the aerator twice a year helps. If your faucet clogs again within months, the scale may be coming from an older water heater or old galvanized lines in the house, and a closer look is worth the time.

What a drip costs

According to the EPA, a faucet that drips at the rate of one drip per second can waste more than 3,000 gallons per year. A cartridge or washer usually costs far less than a year of that water and the sewer charge that rides with it.
