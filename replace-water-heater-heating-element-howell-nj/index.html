Replacing a Heating Element in a Hot Water Tank in Howell, NJ

An electric water heater has two heating elements, one near the top and one near the bottom. When one fails, you get lukewarm water, short hot showers or no hot water at all. Replacing an element is one of the more common repairs, and it is within reach of a careful homeowner.

This guide explains how to tell an element is bad, the safe steps to replace it and the signs that mean the tank itself is finished. It is written for Howell, NJ homes with an electric tank.

Is it the element or something else

Lukewarm water is the classic symptom of a failed lower element, because the upper element heats only the top of the tank. No hot water at all can mean a failed upper element, a tripped high limit switch, a bad thermostat or a tripped breaker. Always check the simple things before you open the panel.

Safety comes first

An electric water heater runs on 240 volt power. Turn off the breaker for the heater, then confirm with a tester that the power is off at the heater before you touch any wire. If you are not comfortable with that, stop and call us. A shock hazard is not worth the savings.

Testing an element

With the power off, remove the access panel and the insulation, and take off the plastic cover. Disconnect the wires from one element. A multimeter set to ohms should read a low resistance, often in the range of 10 to 16 ohms for a typical household element. An open reading means the element has failed. A reading from the element to the metal tank means it has shorted.

Replacing an element step by step

Power off and drain

Turn off the breaker, shut the cold supply, open a hot tap and drain the tank below the element level.

Remove the old element

Take off the wires, then use an element wrench to unscrew it. A stuck element may need penetrating oil and patience.

Clean the area

Remove the old gasket and scrape the opening, and rinse out the sediment you see.

Install and refill

Fit the new element with a new gasket, reconnect the wires, close the drain, refill the tank and open a hot tap until water flows with no air before you turn the breaker on.

Never turn the power back on before the tank is full. A dry element burns out in seconds. This is the most common mistake we see, and it costs a new element and sometimes a new thermostat as well.

Buy the right part

  • Match the wattage and voltage printed on the heater data plate.
  • Check the element style, screw in or bolt in.
  • Buy a new gasket if the element does not include one.
  • Check whether your heater has one element or two.
  • Consider replacing both elements if one is old.
  • Pick up an element wrench from a hardware store.

When the tank is the problem

If the tank is old, rusted at the base or leaking at the bottom, a new element will not save it. A tank that has already lasted around a decade rarely justifies a part and an afternoon. Test the heater after the repair

After the tank is full and the breaker is on, wait about an hour and check the water at a hot tap.

Feel the upper and lower sections of the tank through the access panels without touching any wire. Listen for dripping or hissing. If hot water returns and the breaker holds, the repair worked. If the water stays cold, the thermostat or the high limit switch may be the cause, and the element may not be the only part that failed.

Do not keep resetting a tripping breaker. Repeated trips mean a fault that needs a closer look, and our team can find it quickly.

Safety is the last point. Never test a live element with the power on, never bypass a limit switch and never leave the access panel off. These parts exist to prevent overheating and fire, and a repair that removes a safeguard is not a repair.

Howell note: why scale kills elements here

New Jersey American Water's 2025 water quality report for its Coastal North system lists Howell as a distribution sampling location and shows total hardness of 72 to 140 mg/L. Hardness at that level leaves a crust on a heating element over time, and a crusted element runs hotter, wears faster and burns out sooner.

A yearly tank flush slows that buildup. If your water comes from a private well, a test will show hardness and iron, and a softener or filter may be worth asking about. If you want help, our crews also work in Lakewood and nearby towns on the same basis.

Sources
