Start with the symptom

Most people never search for leak detection until something odd happens. The symptom tells us where to look first, and a short test at home can narrow the search before we arrive.

Meter keeps moving

Shut off every tap and appliance, then watch the meter dial or leak indicator. Movement means water is leaving the pipes somewhere.

High water bill

A bill that climbs for weeks with no change in habits often means a running toilet, an irrigation leak or a slow pipe leak.

Wet ceiling or wall

Water follows framing, so the stain is rarely under the leak. We trace it back along the pipes above.

Warm spot on the floor

A hot water line leaking under a slab or in a floor can warm the surface above it.

Do this before you call

  • Turn off all water and note the meter reading, then check again after 30 minutes.
  • Test toilets by dropping food coloring in the tank and waiting 15 minutes without flushing.
  • Close the main shutoff and see if the meter still moves, which points to a leak outside the house.
  • Look at the water heater, washing machine hoses and under sinks for drips.

How we find a hidden leak

We use several methods and cross check them. Pressure testing isolates a section of pipe and shows if it holds. Acoustic listening picks up the hiss of escaping water through floors and soil. Thermal imaging shows temperature differences that mark a hot water leak or damp insulation. A moisture meter confirms wet drywall or subfloor. A camera inspects drain lines from the inside.

Slab, crawlspace and buried line leaks

Slab leaks are more of a concern in some warmer states, and many New Jersey homes sit over basements or crawlspaces, but homes with slab-on-grade floors exist. In a basement, a leak shows as damp block, efflorescence or a musty smell. In a crawlspace, we check insulation and floor joists for moisture.

An underground service line leak may surface as a wet patch in the yard or a pressure drop through the whole house, and we cover that repair on the water service line repair page.

Irrigation lines are another source. A broken lawn sprinkler head or a cracked line can run for days and inflate a bill. We test the system separately so the leak is not blamed on the house plumbing.

Ceiling and wall leaks

A ceiling leak under a bathroom is usually a shower pan, a supply line or a drain, and one under a roof is usually weather. We separate the two by testing fixtures and checking the attic. If the water source is a failed fitting behind a finished wall, we cut the smallest access hole we can, repair the pipe and tell you what a drywall patch will involve.

Howell note: your utility meter helps the search

In municipal water areas of Howell, the water meter is your best first tool. New Jersey American Water serves part of Howell through its Coastal North system, and its Yellowbrook Water Treatment Plant sits in the township. If the meter moves with everything off, the leak is somewhere between the meter and the fixtures.

Homes on private wells have no utility meter, so a leak shows as a pump that cycles too often or a loss of pressure. In either case, tell us whether your house is on public water or a well, because it changes how we test the system.

Why a small leak is worth chasing

According to the EPA's WaterSense program, the average household's leaks can waste more than 9,300 gallons of water every year. The EPA suggests checking your water meter before and after a two hour period when no water is being used, which is a stricter version of the test described above.
